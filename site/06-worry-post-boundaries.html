Chapter 06

6. Boundaries on AI worry posts

Part of calm following AI is deciding, in advance, how much worry content you’ll let into your week. Worry posts are a genre now: existential risk threads, “AI will take your job” essays, screenshots of researchers sounding alarmed. Some of it is serious intellectual work worth knowing about. All of it is highly engaging and zero-actionable for most readers most days — which is a dangerous ratio for your attention.

These are the boundaries. Adopt them whole, adjust to taste, but have them written down before the next alarming thing shows up.

Boundary 1 — Time-boxed, not ambient

Worry content is allowed at most one scheduled slot a week, not scattered through days. Ambient worry is the doomscroll trap: one thread at breakfast, one at lunch, each harmless alone, together a mood. If a post is genuinely important, it will survive until your slot. Put it in a “worry, later” bookmark folder and read the folder once, on the clock, one session a week.

Boundary 2 — Read claims, not moods

In the slot, read the actual sentences: what claim, by whom, with what evidence, dated when? Fear spreads through tone; information travels in specifics. A post like “researcher warns we can’t understand what models are doing” becomes “Model chip X’s internal reasoning is hard to interpret; one team proposes method Y” — knowable, and much less alarming because it’s checkable.

Boundary 3 — Separate “informed” from “obliged”

You’re allowed to know about AI risks and still not solve them this month. Being concerned is not a homework assignment. There are people and institutions whose job is mitigation; your legal duty as a curious reader ends at being accurately informed and maybe voting/organizing if that’s your thing. What you owe yourself is not letting it bleed into every scroll session.

Boundary 4 — Never engage hot takes cold

Don’t argue in comments or quote-posts about AI risk/unrisk the same hour you read the take. Almost no argument on this topic is won same-day, and the engagement feeds the dispenser. If you must reply, wait 24 hours. Your calmer voice is better anyway, and usually by then the thread has moved on, which is the true use of the waiting period.

Boundary 5 — Unfollow the anxiety-optimized accounts

Curate your feed like a toolbox: if an account leaves you worried-but-uninformed more than once a week, unfollow. You keep sources for information (Chapter 1), not sources for affects. This is a filter, not an echo chamber — you unfollow for how they make you feel, and you can still read careful critical voices that don’t trade in dread.

Boundary 6 — Your worry has a note file, not a feed

If you’re genuinely concerned about something specific (data privacy, energy use, job impact), put it in your notes with one next concrete step you control (“encrypt my note backups,” “update résumé sections,” “donate once a year”). A worry that gets converted into a file with a task stops being a scroll trigger. Zero-actionable worry is the only kind worth dropping entirely — and it’s most of it, honestly.

Boundary 7 — Protect people around you too

Friends and family will forward the scary post. You don’t have to debate it. A stock response helps: “Wild if true — I’ll read it in my Sunday AI slot. If it’s real, we’ll likely know within a week.” That’s kind, non-dismissive, and buys the week in which most panic posts quietly die.

Why boundaries, not bans

You shouldn’t ignore AI’s real changes and risks; that’s its own form of information loss. Boundaries exist to make worry scarce and deliberate instead of constant and reflexive. You decide when and how much, on the clock, from sources you’ve chosen. That’s the entire difference between “keeping up” and being kept up at night.

The one-paragraph version: one weekly slot, read claims not tones, convert worries to action-files or drop them, never engage hot, and unfollow anyone who profits from your pulse. Worry posts are an event you attend, not a place you live.
