Chapter 04

4. One experiment a month

Panic-upgrading tools is what happens when following AI feels like homework graded daily. The cure isn’t ignoring tools — it’s scheduling them. One deliberate experiment per month beats twelve panicked ones per year, because a monthly experiment is actually finished.

The habit

Once a month — same day each month, calendar it — you run one controlled trial: one new tool, one new model, or one new workflow, against the thing you currently use. It takes an afternoon at most. You write down three sentences. Then you either adopt it, keep what you have, or table it.

Nothing else counts. If a big release lands mid-month, that’s fine — it joins the queue for next month’s experiment slot. You’re never reacting; you’re scheduling.

The three parts of a monthly experiment

  1. A real task, not a demo. Experiment on something you’d genuinely do anyway: write a newsletter draft, summarize a document, fix a script. Demos flatter tools; your actual workload doesn’t.
  2. An equal fight. Run the new tool side-by-side with your current one, same task, same day. Ten minutes each is plenty — enthusiasm fades fast but quality shows up early.
  3. A written verdict. Three sentences in a note file: - Kept/switched/dropped? - Best thing it did. - What I still had to fix by hand.

That note is your tool knowledge. In a year you’ll have twelve entries and you’ll know, from evidence, what actually improved your week — a claim almost nobody can make who doomscrolled the whole time.

Picking the month’s experiment (ordered by what usually wins)

  1. A thing that automates a chore you did 4+ times this month. Highest expected payoff.
  2. The new version of something you already use (upgrade your assistant model, VS Code, or whatever you deployed). Cheap to test: same habits, new quality.
  3. One open-weight model you could run locally if you’re curious about local AI (see the Massive AI Manual’s local AI guide).
  4. A workflow pattern, not a product: “ruthlessly use one assistant for all my drafts this month” counts as an experiment.

The queue

Keep a tiny note called experiment-queue.md. When news lands that might matter, one line: “March: try the new voice mode for my commute notes.” That’s the entire discipline — capture, defer, forget. News gets held instead of felt. This pairs with Chapter 2’s minute 5.

Rules that keep it calm

  • Skip months are allowed. A month where nothing’s worth testing is a good month — adding tests costs attention too.
  • No chains. One experiment. If it introduces another thing, that thing goes in the queue, not in your weekend.
  • Reversibility matters. Prefer experiments you can walk away from. Keep your old tool installed during the trial month.
  • Miracle claims wait a month. Overnight sensations are mostly resolved by the community within two weeks. Your monthly slot naturally waits that out.

What this looks like over a year

  • Jan: switched newsletter drafts to the new assistant model (faster, kept formatting rules).
  • Feb: dropped a tab-manager extension; my notes app covers it.
  • Mar: skipped — nothing notable.
  • Apr: added a local model for private document summaries.
  • May: kept my coding tool after testing the challenger (close race, switching costs lost).

Four decisions, twelve entries of evidence, zero panic. That’s the whole point.
